# ## :wave: About
This repository contains the code and results for the paper "Disentangling the drivers of parasite occurrence in a native and a non-native small mammal across a land-use gradient".

## :page_facing_up: Paper and citing


## Abstract:
Land-use change can reorganize host--parasite systems by altering environmental conditions, reshaping native and non-native host communities, and changing transmission pathways. Yet parasite occurrence remains difficult to predict, as it depends simultaneously on habitat conditions, the host species, individual host attributes, the within-host microbiome, and parasite biology. Here, we integrate these components into a single analytical framework and quantify their relative contributions to the occurrence of gut parasites in two coexisting small mammals across a land-use gradient in northeastern Madagascar: the native, Malagasy-endemic shrew tenrec, Microgale brevicaudata (Tenrecidae), and the non-native, invasive black rat, Rattus rattus (Muridae). We characterized protozoan and nematode parasites from 333 R. rattus and 172 M. brevicaudata host individuals and combined these data with environmental variables, host attributes, and gut microbiome metrics. We then used Hierarchical Modeling of Species Communities (HMSC) to partition the explained variance in occurrence and to test whether parasite taxonomic group structured parasite responses to these drivers. The host species was the dominant driver overall, accounting for approximately half of the weighted explained variance, followed by environmental conditions. Its importance, however, varied markedly among parasites. The two hosts also responded to the land-use gradient in opposite directions, each harboring its richest parasite community at the opposite end of the gradient---M. brevicaudata in the primary forest and R. rattus in the village---indicating that land-use change shifted parasite richness between the two hosts rather than affecting both in the same direction. Our results show that parasite occurrence in disturbed landscapes emerges from the combined effects of host species, environment, and parasite biology, and that land-use change can shift infection between coexisting native and non-native hosts. This multi-level perspective can improve predictions of infection risk as global change expands the interface between native and non-native hosts.
